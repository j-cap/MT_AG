\chapter{Research Backlog}
\section{Phase 1 completion criteria}
Phase 1 is complete only after the following sequence is documented and reproducible.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{P1A -- IMU baseline:} deterministic planar IMU mechanization, noisy inertial drift, Gaussian UWB measurement, and conventional PF correction. Current status: \textbf{completed}. The implementation is verified and the result is supported by the P1B multi-seed study.
    \item \textbf{P1B -- robustness of the known-pose baseline:} multi-seed study; sensitivity to initial position/velocity/yaw uncertainty; particle count; IMU noise and bias levels; PF process-noise sensitivity. Current status: \textbf{completed and documented}. The known-pose PF improves position RMSE in all 20 baseline seeds, while large initialization errors and persistent IMU bias have been identified as explicit boundaries for later work.
    \item \textbf{P1C -- unknown initial pose:} introduce the global-localization problem using a first-range-conditioned position ring and globally distributed yaw hypotheses. Make the initial velocity prior explicit for the IMU-driven state. Current status: \textbf{completed as a diagnostic phase}. The known-yaw control is largely recoverable, whereas the realistic unknown-position/yaw bootstrap PF converges in only 2/20 core runs. Increasing particle count or disabling resampling does not resolve the failure. An idealized 40000-particle check with exact IMU/UWB information converges in 3/3 runs, showing that the selected moving-auxiliary scenario can resolve the ambiguity under ideal information.
    \item \textbf{P1D -- geometry/observability:} compare stationary, degenerate constant-bearing, and informative moving auxiliary trajectories; report convergence, particle-cloud behaviour, and finite-horizon observability singular values. Current status: \textbf{completed and documented}. The stationary single auxiliary has numerical rank four, the constant-bearing construction rank three, and only the informative moving auxiliary reaches full rank five. The two rank-deficient cases achieve no full-pose convergence in either realistic or idealized filtering, while the moving case is the only geometry with successful recovery. Realistic moving-case success is nevertheless only 5/20, showing that full local observability is necessary but not sufficient for robust bootstrap-PF global localization.
    \item \textbf{P1E -- paper reproduction audit:} document each equation and algorithmic step of Han et al.; list every underspecified AACOPF parameter and the chosen interpretation. Distinguish the paper's $\Delta L,\Delta\psi$ DR interface from the thesis IMU-driven mechanization. Current status: \textbf{completed and documented}. The audit identifies the main source ambiguities, including propagation timing, first-range sampling, the printed likelihood expression, $\alpha/\beta/\lambda$, the weight-difference definition, candidate selection, the undefined particle-rejection condition, adaptive-node thresholds, and the output ordering after uniform weight reset. A machine-readable interpretation contract fixes the P1F implementation choices.
    \item \textbf{P1F -- AACOPF implementation and evaluation:} implement the audited transition mechanism and compare conventional PF and AACOPF under matched conditions. Current status: \textbf{in progress; P1F-A through P1F-D completed; P1F-E next}. P1F-A establishes the paper-state conventional-PF reference. P1F-B verifies the literal-small all-pairs ACO mechanism and exposes aggressive cloning and quadratic scaling. P1F-C performs a 60-setting safety-aware sweep and freezes $(\alpha,\beta,c_\lambda)=(0.5,0,2)$. P1F-D then performs the first held-out equal-budget PF--AACOPF comparison in informative geometry. The primary strict-convergence hypothesis is not supported: at $N_p=1200$, PF converges in $2/20$ runs and AACOPF in $1/20$. AACOPF nevertheless reduces aggregate late position/yaw RMSE, revealing a secondary error-shaping effect. The remaining sequence is P1F-E rank-deficient negative controls, P1F-F non-Gaussian/outlier stress, and P1F-G scalable bounded-candidate adaptation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Current Phase-1 parameter and architecture decisions after P1F-D}
For the local known-pose experiments, the P1B conclusion remains valid: $N_p=5000$ particles is a conservative default, and $N_p=2500$ is acceptable for exploratory sweeps. The P1A PF process-noise values are retained because P1B shows similar performance for the baseline and a $2\times$ scaling, whereas substantially smaller process noise causes under-dispersion and occasional failures.

The IMU-driven global-pose problem requires a different interpretation of particle count. A structured $100\times100$ position/yaw grid ($N_p=10000$) remains the practical global-search baseline for realistic matched diagnostic experiments. P1C showed that increasing this grid to $150\times150$ did not make convergence reliable. The idealized $200\times200$ ($N_p=40000$) configurations are therefore diagnostic tests of representability/identifiability rather than practical defaults.

P1D establishes that geometry must be treated before particle-algorithm tuning. The stationary single-auxiliary and constant-bearing cases are rank deficient and therefore serve as negative controls: no particle-transition strategy should be interpreted as solving the missing-information problem in those geometries. The informative moving case is full rank but remains difficult for the bootstrap PF; this is the principal geometry for testing particle-management algorithms.

P1E fixes a separate paper-level baseline. Han et al. are reproduced first with the three-state $[x,y,\phi]$ model and higher-level $\Delta L,\Delta\phi$ inputs. The primary propagation convention follows their formal Equation~(3), while the Algorithm~1 post-turn convention is retained as a sensitivity variant. Random first-range annular initialization is used for the paper-level reproduction; the annulus width $\Delta d=3\sigma_{\mathrm{UWB}}$ is an explicit repository choice because the paper does not report $\Delta d$.

P1F-A fixes the practical conventional-PF reference at $N_p=10000$, with $N_p=40000$ retained as an upper-population reference. Correct-region hypotheses are present initially even in many failing runs, so the key difficulty is survival and selection of the useful mode under sequential likelihood weighting and particle replacement.

The primary AACOPF interpretation uses a Gaussian range likelihood, strictly higher-weight destination candidates, weight difference $w_j-w_i$, synchronous movement from the pre-transition cloud, pre-ACO posterior output, and a uniform post-transition weight reset. P1F-B initially evaluated $(\alpha,\beta,c_\lambda)=(1,1,0.5)$ as a mechanism-check point and showed that this configuration is too aggressive for reliable use.

P1F-C freezes the literal-small comparison tuple
\begin{equation}
    (\alpha,\beta,c_\lambda)=(0.5,0,2).
\end{equation}
The tuple is selected from 60 candidates using 16 matched development runs and then evaluated on 40 held-out controlled bimodal runs. Held-out correct-mode lock is 40/40 and wrong-mode lock is 0/40. Catastrophic ancestry collapse still occurs in 9/40 runs and a dominant-clone event in 34/40 runs. Therefore this setting is accepted only as a frozen parameterization for held-out comparison; it is not considered diversity-safe or deployment-ready.

P1F-D demonstrates the limits of transferring this controlled tuning to the complete random-annulus global problem. At $N_p=1200$, the random initial cloud contains only 2.35 particles in the diagnostic correct region on average, compared with 80 or 200 correct-mode particles in the P1F-C tuning scenarios. Under this sparse-support condition, conventional PF succeeds in $2/20$ runs and AACOPF in $1/20$, so the primary convergence-improvement hypothesis is not supported. At the same time, AACOPF reduces mean late position RMSE from \SI{19.945}{m} to \SI{12.782}{m} and mean late yaw RMSE from $66.95^\circ$ to $33.99^\circ$. This is retained as a secondary error-shaping effect rather than a global-convergence claim.

The P1F-D result adds an important algorithmic boundary: an ACO copying operator can amplify a useful mode only if that mode has adequate particle support. It cannot create absent hypotheses, and a mode represented by only one or two particles remains easy to eliminate. Retuning the frozen parameters on the P1F-D seeds is explicitly disallowed; any mechanism intended to improve sparse-mode survival belongs to a new experiment.

The computational distinction between literal reproduction and later deployment remains unchanged. Han et al. recommend $N=M=400$, corresponding to $160000$ particles. A literal dense interpretation contains $2.56\times10^{10}$ pair entries; one \texttt{float64} pair matrix alone requires approximately \SI{204.8}{GB}. P1F-D further shows that at $N_p=1200$ the current literal AACOPF requires about \SI{47.1}{s} per run versus \SI{0.248}{s} for conventional PF. The literal implementation is therefore restricted to mechanism validation and manageable populations.

Persistent accelerometer/gyroscope bias remains an open estimator-design question rather than an immediately added state. The P1B stress test shows that unestimated bias can dominate the remaining error, but the selected stress levels are synthetic. The decision to augment the state with bias variables should therefore be made after real IMU bias/noise characteristics are available.

The initial velocity assumption also remains explicit in the IMU-driven branch. P1C shows that free initial velocity substantially worsens the global search. The aligned fixed-speed prior is therefore retained through the five-state Phase-1 diagnostic studies so that the separate initial-velocity problem is not confounded with observability and particle management. The paper-level three-state P1F branch does not introduce velocity states because Han et al. absorb motion into $\Delta L$ and $\Delta\phi$.

\section{Immediate P1F sequence after P1F-D}
The next experiment is \textbf{P1F-E}. It retains the stationary and constant-bearing geometries from P1D as rank-deficient negative controls and applies the same frozen AACOPF tuple $(0.5,0,2)$ without retuning. The scientific purpose is to verify that the particle-transition mechanism does not appear to overcome genuine missing observability. Success in such geometries would be a warning sign that the metric or implementation is misleading rather than evidence that AACOPF repairs non-observability.

P1F-F then introduces outlier/non-Gaussian ranging to test the robustness motivation emphasized by Han et al. P1F-G replaces the dense destination set by a bounded/scalable candidate mechanism and must quantify both the accuracy--diversity--runtime trade-off and the sparse-support problem exposed by P1F-D. In particular, P1F-G should consider whether bounded candidate selection alone is sufficient or whether explicit particle rejuvenation/support-preservation is needed.

\section{Paper-reproduction architecture after P1F-D}
The earlier Option-A/Option-B decision remains resolved in favor of \textbf{two baselines}. The paper-level reproduction uses $[x,y,\phi]$ with $\Delta L,\Delta\phi$ so that the AACOPF mechanism can be assessed without the additional initialization problem introduced by velocity states. The hardware-oriented thesis estimator remains $[p_x,p_y,v_x,v_y,\psi]$ with raw IMU mechanization. Only after the paper-level AACOPF and its scalable adaptation are understood will a selected particle-management concept be transferred to the IMU-driven estimator.

The P1E audit fixes the claim boundary. Exact numerical replication of Han et al. is not required because raw trajectories, ranging data, random seeds, update frequency, complete noise distributions, and several AACOPF tuning parameters are unavailable. Phase 1 instead requires a transparent qualitative reproduction of the problem structure, observability behavior, and audited particle-management mechanism under fully documented assumptions. P1F-C/P1F-D strengthen this boundary: the frozen parameters are explicitly repository-selected, and the held-out comparison is reported even though its primary result is negative.

\section{Phase 2--4 outlook}
Phase 2 will vary UWB update rate, node count, geometry, outages, LOS/NLOS degradation, and IMU quality. P1D establishes that range count alone is not a sufficient measure of localization information, while P1F-D adds that particle support and algorithmic mode preservation must be tracked when communication conditions change. Phase 3 will develop uncertainty/information-aware UWB scheduling and node selection. Phase 4 will characterize the actual hardware, replace simulated sensor parameters with measured ones, measure energy per UWB transaction, port the selected estimator to C/C++, and compare simulation and hardware.
